\begin{abstract}

General-purpose vision--language models (VLMs) offer one interface for
heterogeneous medical image analysis, but adapting a single pretrained model
to tasks with different visual, semantic and output requirements raises an
open design question: which adaptation parameters should be shared across
tasks, and which should be specialized? We study this question with
low-rank adaptation (LoRA) on six task families (classification,
multi-label classification, detection, counting, regression and report
generation) drawn from 17 datasets and 7 imaging modalities, using two
backbones, a general VLM (Qwen3.5-9B) and a medical VLM (MedGemma-1.5-4B).
Under one fixed training and evaluation protocol with three seeds, we compare
five arms that vary only the degree of sharing: a fully shared adapter,
task-specific adapters, HydraLoRA with a shared input projection and
task-specific output projections, its reverse, and a shared adapter with six
times the rank as a parameter-matched control. We further measure cross-task
transfer, compare specialization by task, imaging modality and dataset, test
whether specialization survives predicted rather than oracle routing, relate
transfer to representation similarity, and examine how the amount of
task-specific supervision changes the preferred degree of sharing.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{G}{eneral-purpose} vision--language models (VLMs) have emerged
as a promising unified framework for medical image analysis. A single model
can potentially support disease classification, multi-label recognition,
lesion localization, quantitative estimation, counting and clinical report
generation through a common image--text interface. This flexibility is
attractive for building general-purpose medical artificial intelligence
systems, but it also brings substantial heterogeneity in visual
requirements, output spaces and supervision regimes.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), in particular Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora}, adapts large pretrained VLMs without updating the
full model. Heterogeneous medical tasks turn this into a design choice. At
one extreme, a single shared adapter is trained on all tasks, maximizing
data sharing but risking cross-task interference. At the other, independent
task-specific adapters avoid interference but restrict each adapter to its
own task's supervision. Partially shared designs such as
HydraLoRA lie between the two.

Existing approaches make different assumptions about how specialization
should be structured: some separate downstream tasks explicitly, some share
low-rank components, some introduce modality-specific
experts~\cite{jiang2024medmoe}. They are usually evaluated in isolation,
with different data, prompts and budgets, which makes it hard to tell which
properties of a medical task favor sharing and which favor specialization.

The central question of this study is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{When adapting a general-purpose VLM to heterogeneous medical imaging
tasks, which adaptation parameters should be shared and which should be
specialized?}
\end{quote}

Rather than proposing a new method, we hold one protocol fixed and vary only
the degree and level of sharing. Our testbed covers six task families that
differ in output structure, from a single class label to free-text reports
(Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). The study answers four research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
    \item Does task-specific adaptation outperform shared adaptation, for
    which task families, and does any gain remain against a shared adapter
    with the same number of parameters?

    \item How does the amount of task-specific supervision change the
    preferred degree of sharing?

    \item What determines whether tasks help or hurt each other: output
    format, imaging modality, dataset, or the similarity of their inputs?

    \item Which measurable task characteristics predict whether a task
    benefits from shared or specialized adaptation?
\end{enumerate}
% RQ wording: matches PLAN.md RQ1--RQ4 and Tausifa's revised list, with three
% clarifications agreed for v2: RQ1 "capacity matched" = the Shared r48
% control; RQ3 "visual content" = input similarity (E7); RQ4 predictors
% include quantities measured on trained adapters (E3 transferability and
% malleability, E7 adapter similarity), not only before training.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a controlled comparison of five sharing arms on two backbones, with
    three seeds and a parameter-matched shared control (RQ1);
    \item a cross-task transfer matrix and a comparison of specialization by
    task, modality and dataset, including a natural experiment in which one
    ultrasound dataset carries three task types on the same images (RQ3);
    \item an analysis of which task characteristics, including input and
    adapter representation similarity, predict the benefit of
    specialization (RQ4);
    \item a supervision-scaling study of the sharing--specialization
    trade-off (RQ2).
\end{itemize}

\section{The Sharing--Specialization Spectrum}
\label{sec:spectrum}

Let a pretrained VLM be parameterized by $\theta_0$. For task
$t\in\mathcal{T}$, parameter-efficient adaptation introduces an update
$\Delta\theta_t$ such that
\begin{equation}
f_t(x,q)=f(x,q;\theta_0+\Delta\theta_t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ denotes the medical image(s) and $q$ the textual question. For a
LoRA-adapted linear layer,
\begin{equation}
W_t=W_0+\Delta W_t,\qquad \Delta W_t=\frac{\alpha}{r}B_tA_t,
\end{equation}
where $A_t\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ projects the input down to rank $r$ and
$B_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d'\times r}$ maps it back up. The question is how
$A_t$ and $B_t$ should be shared across tasks. We compare five arms
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

\subsection{Shared LoRA}
One adapter is trained on all tasks: $\Delta W_t=BA$ for every $t$.

\subsection{Task-specific LoRA (TaskLoRA)}
Six independent adapters, one per task family: $\Delta W_t=B_tA_t$.

\subsection{HydraLoRA with task heads (Hydra-T)}
A shared input projection and one output head per task:
$\Delta W_t=B_tA$. Tasks share input features but keep their own output
mapping.

\subsection{Reversed HydraLoRA (Hydra-R)}
One input projection per task and a shared output projection:
$\Delta W_t=BA_t$. Comparing Hydra-T with Hydra-R asks whether it matters
\emph{which} matrix is shared, or only how much is shared.

\subsection{Shared LoRA, rank 48 (control)}
A shared adapter with $r=48=6\times 8$ and $\alpha=96$, so that it stores
about as many parameters as TaskLoRA and keeps the same scale $\alpha/r=2$.
It separates a gain from specialization from a gain from having six times
the parameters.

\subsection{Specialization by modality and by dataset}
The same TaskLoRA construction with a different grouping: one adapter per
imaging modality (trained on all tasks of that modality), or, within
classification, one adapter per dataset. These ask at which level sharing
should be cut: task, modality or dataset.

Specialized arms need a route at test time. Unless stated otherwise we use
the oracle route (the known task, modality or dataset label);
Section~\ref{sec:e6} replaces it with a predicted route.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Tasks and Data}

We use the FLARE 2026 Task~3 data~\cite{flare2026task3}: 17 datasets across
seven imaging modalities and six scored task families
(Table~\ref{tab:tasks}, Table~\ref{tab:data}). Instance detection is
excluded because it has no evaluation metric, and endoscopy (15 training
questions) is excluded from training and evaluation. Questions are bare: the
dataset's question as given, with no system prompt and no added
instruction. A question may carry up to four images.

The training pool contains 40{,}094 questions. All results are reported on a
fixed, image-disjoint holdout of 4{,}447 questions, which forms 19
(task, dataset) cells. Each finished arm is evaluated on the holdout once; no
model selection uses it.

\subsection{Backbones}

\textbf{Qwen3.5-9B}~\cite{qwen2026qwen35}, a general-purpose VLM, is the
primary backbone; images are capped at a $768^2$ pixel area.
\textbf{MedGemma-1.5-4B}~\cite{sellergren2025medgemma}, a medically
pretrained VLM, is the second; its SigLIP encoder takes a fixed $896^2$
input, so the $768^2$ cap cannot apply. Both are loaded in 4-bit NF4 with
BF16 compute; the vision encoder, the projector and all base weights are
frozen. Adapters wrap every linear layer of the language model (248 on
Qwen, 238 on MedGemma).

\subsection{Training and Inference Protocol}

All arms share one recipe, with no per-arm tuning: LoRA rank 8, $\alpha=16$,
dropout 0.05 (except Shared r48); AdamW, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$ with
linear decay and 3\% warmup, three epochs, effective batch 8
($1\times8$ gradient accumulation); the final checkpoint is used. The loss is
token cross-entropy averaged within each example and then across examples,
so a long report weighs the same as a one-token label. Mixtures are sampled
in their natural proportions, with no task or dataset balancing. Every arm
is trained with seeds 1337, 2026 and 42. Training uses
LLaMA-Factory~\cite{zheng2024llamafactory}; all runs that enter a comparison
are trained and evaluated on the same GPU, because we found training was
not bit-reproducible across machines.

Inference is greedy, with the end-of-sequence tokens pinned, the Qwen
thinking mode disabled and at most 512 new tokens. We log the malformed-
and truncated-output rates for every run.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Anchors}

Two zero-shot protocols give the base-model reference (E0): \emph{ZS-bare},
the bare question, and \emph{ZS-prompt}, the same question with one
task-agnostic system prompt (``Answer the question about the medical
image(s). Return only the requested answer, without an explanation.''). The
difference between them shows how much of the zero-shot gap is output
format. ZS-prompt is the base for the transfer score and the normalized
score $\mu$ below.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}

Each metric is computed per dataset and then averaged, unweighted, over the
datasets of a task; we never pool predictions across datasets. For
aggregate plots and for E8 only, we normalize against the zero-shot base
$M_{\mathrm{base}}$: $\mu=(M-M_{\mathrm{base}})/(1-M_{\mathrm{base}})$ for
higher-is-better metrics and
$\mu=(\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}}-\mathrm{MAE})/\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{base}}$
for MAE~\cite{sachdeva2026tasktransfer}.

The CRIMSON judge occasionally returns no score for a report. This never
happened for a fine-tuned arm's own report outputs; it happened for 13 of the
781 MedGemma ZS-prompt reports, 1 Qwen ZS-bare report, and up to 13 reports
per run when a non-report adapter answers report questions (E3). Such reports
are left out of the CRIMSON mean, and we give their count wherever it is
non-zero.

We report the mean and standard deviation over three seeds. Confidence
intervals come from a two-level bootstrap (seeds, then holdout questions,
stratified by dataset, 10{,}000 resamples, with the task macro recomputed
on each resample). Arm differences use a paired bootstrap on the same
questions, with Holm correction across the six tasks within each
comparison.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{E0--E2: The Five Arms on Two Backbones (RQ1)}
\label{sec:e1}

Every arm of Table~\ref{tab:arms} is trained on the full mixture with each
seed and evaluated on the whole holdout (E1 on Qwen, E2 on MedGemma). The
contrasts, each per task, are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item TaskLoRA $-$ Shared: does specialization help, and for which task
    families?
    \item TaskLoRA $-$ Shared r48: is a gain due to specialization or to six
    times the parameters?
    \item Hydra-T $-$ Hydra-R: does it matter which matrix is shared?
    \item Hydra-T and Hydra-R against Shared and TaskLoRA: where partial
    sharing falls between the extremes.
\end{itemize}
For each backbone $b$ we summarize the effect of specialization as
$\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(b)}=M_{\mathrm{TaskLoRA}}^{(b)}-M_{\mathrm{Shared}}^{(b)}$
per task, read alongside the differences in model size, input resolution
and pretraining between the backbones. We also report an output-format error
table: whether a shared arm ever answers in another task's format.

\subsection{E3: Cross-Task Transfer (RQ3, RQ4)}
\label{sec:e3}

Each task adapter $i$ is evaluated on every other task $j$'s holdout slice,
giving a $6\times6$ matrix per seed and backbone. The transfer score is
\begin{equation}
C_{ij}=M_j(\mathrm{TaskLoRA}_i)-M_j(\mathrm{base}),
\end{equation}
with metrics oriented so that larger is better and the base being
ZS-prompt (recomputed against ZS-bare, listing the cells whose sign flips).
We also report the normalized transfer $\mu_{i\to j}$, each source task's
transferability and each target task's malleability~\cite{sachdeva2026tasktransfer},
and the malformed-output rate per cell, since locking into the source task's
output format is a likely mechanism of negative transfer.
The report column scores non-report adapters' answers to report questions,
which are often not reports at all; we keep and flag these cells but draw no
conclusion from their CRIMSON values.

We relate $C_{ij}$ over the 30 off-diagonal pairs to output-structure
similarity, modality overlap, dataset overlap and source and target data
size, using Spearman correlations with permutation tests. One dataset (IUGC)
carries classification, detection and regression questions on the same
ultrasound images; transfer among those three tasks holds modality and
dataset fixed and varies only the output format.

\subsection{E4--E5: Specialization by Modality and by Dataset (RQ3)}
\label{sec:e4}

E4 trains one adapter per imaging modality on all of that modality's
questions, whatever the task (seven modalities). E5 trains one adapter per
classification dataset (ten). Both are trained with the recipe of
Section~\ref{sec:setup} on both backbones with three seeds. Each adapter is
evaluated on its own slice with the oracle route; joining the slices covers
the whole holdout (E4) or every classification question (E5), which we
compare on the same questions with Shared, TaskLoRA and the other arms.
Three modalities hold a single classification dataset, so
their E4 and E5 adapters are identical and are trained once. For E5 we
relate the per-dataset gain over TaskLoRA-cls to dataset size and zero-shot
difficulty.

\subsection{E6: Predicted Routing}
\label{sec:e6}

E1--E5 route with the known label. E6 replaces it with a predicted one: a
question-only text classifier~\cite{stepanov2025gliclass} for the task route
(TaskLoRA, Hydra-T, Hydra-R) and an image classifier for the modality route
(E4). We report routing accuracy and the end-to-end metric drop against the
oracle route: does the gain from specialization survive without labels?

\subsection{E7: Representations (RQ3, RQ4)}
\label{sec:e7}

On a fixed, stratified probe set of 30 holdout questions per cell, we
extract mean-pooled image-token and question-token features and the last
prompt token at a fixed set of language-model layers, for the base model and
every arm. We measure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{input similarity}: representational similarity between the
    base model's representations of source task $i$'s inputs and target cell
    $c$'s inputs, correlated with transfer $C_{i,c}$ (permutation test, Holm
    across layers). This is the main test;
    \item \emph{adapter similarity}: debiased linear CKA between task
    adapters on the same inputs, against $C_{ij}$ (descriptive only);
    \item \emph{shift from base}: CKA between each model and the base, per
    layer, to locate where specialization appears;
    \item \emph{Hydra heads}: pairwise similarity of the task heads ($B_t$
    in Hydra-T, $A_t$ in Hydra-R), to see which matrix actually diverges
    between tasks.
\end{itemize}
For representation matrices $X$ and $Y$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CKA}(X,Y)=\frac{\mathrm{HSIC}(X,Y)}
{\sqrt{\mathrm{HSIC}(X,X)\,\mathrm{HSIC}(Y,Y)}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{E8: What Predicts the Benefit of Specialization (RQ4)}
\label{sec:e8}

The unit is the 19 holdout cells, per backbone; the outcome is the
specialization benefit per cell (TaskLoRA $-$ Shared, and each Hydra arm
$-$ Shared), averaged over seeds. Candidate predictors are the cell's and
its task's training size, the number of datasets in the task, the output
structure, the modality, the zero-shot score, the malleability and
transferability from E3, and the input and adapter similarity from E7. With
19 cells we report Spearman correlations with permutation tests per
predictor rather than a joint regression, and check that the sign agrees
across backbones.

\subsection{E9: Supervision Scaling (RQ2)}
\label{sec:e9}

For classification and regression, we train on nested subsets of
$p\in\{1,5,10,25,50,100\}\%$ of the target task's image groups, stratified by
dataset, with the other tasks kept at 100\%. At each $p$ we compare TaskLoRA
(trained on the subset only) with Shared and Hydra-T (the mixture with the
subsetted target). We fit
\begin{equation}
\mu=\beta_0+\beta_1\log N+\beta_2S+\beta_3(\log N\times S)+u_{\mathrm{dataset}}+\epsilon,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of target training examples and $S$ the arm, with
TaskLoRA as the reference; the main test is $\beta_3$. We also estimate the
crossover $p^*$ at which TaskLoRA matches Shared.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Zero-Shot Anchors (E0)}

Both base models are far from usable zero-shot
(Tables~\ref{tab:qwen}--\ref{tab:medgemma}). Much of the gap is output
format rather than capability: with the bare question, Qwen3.5-9B often
answers classification and detection questions in a form the scorer cannot
parse (balanced accuracy 0.004, detection F1 0.000), and a single
task-agnostic instruction lifts classification to 0.118 and multi-label to
0.501. MedGemma-1.5-4B is weaker zero-shot than Qwen on every task except
regression with the prompt.

\subsection{Shared Versus Task-Specific Adaptation (E1, E2)}

paired tests. The text below is descriptive (mean $\pm$ SD over seeds).}

Fine-tuning lifts every task far above zero-shot on both backbones. Reports
gain the least (CRIMSON 0.797 zero-shot to about 0.82--0.83 on Qwen).
Every fine-tuned run produced well-formed, untruncated outputs on every task.

\textbf{TaskLoRA versus Shared.} On Qwen the two arms are close
(Table~\ref{tab:qwen}). Shared is ahead on classification ($-0.012$ balanced
accuracy for TaskLoRA), detection ($-0.011$ F1) and counting (TaskLoRA
$+2.07$ MAE); TaskLoRA is ahead on regression ($-0.72$ MAE, all from bone
resorption) and report generation ($+0.006$ CRIMSON, with fewer omitted
abnormal findings); multi-label is tied. Each gap is about 1--1.5 seed
standard deviations. On MedGemma (Table~\ref{tab:medgemma}), TaskLoRA leans
ahead: detection $+0.060$ F1 on every seed (from the two breast-ultrasound
datasets), multi-label $+0.014$, regression $-0.25$ MAE and reports $+0.007$
CRIMSON, while Shared stays ahead on counting (TaskLoRA $+3.9$ MAE) and,
marginally, classification. The ranking of the two arms therefore depends
on the backbone (Table~\ref{tab:dspec}).

\textbf{The parameter-matched control.} Shared r48, with six times the
parameters of Shared, is no better than Shared on any task on either
backbone, and is worse on counting and report omission on both. Where
TaskLoRA beats Shared, the gain is not explained by its larger parameter
count.

\textbf{Backbones.} With the same arms, MedGemma is close to Qwen on
classification, regression and reports, and behind on multi-label, counting
(about twice the MAE) and detection (0.47 vs 0.90 F1). The detection gap
comes from the breast-ultrasound datasets (0.16--0.32 vs about 0.85 F1;
IUGC is 0.98). Predicted box centres and widths match the ground truth on
average, but MedGemma's horizontal placement correlates poorly with the
ground truth on these wide images. \todo{Test whether resizing to the fixed

\subsection{Modality and Dataset Specialization (E4, E5)}
\todo{Add the Hydra-T and Hydra-R comparisons once their holdout evaluations

\textbf{By modality (E4).} Seven modality adapters perform about as well as
Shared and TaskLoRA (Table~\ref{tab:e4}). No task differs from either after
Holm correction on either backbone. The largest gap is MedGemma multi-label,
where the modality adapters trail TaskLoRA by 0.025 F1 (95\% CI
$[-0.044,-0.007]$, Holm $p=0.06$). Specializing by imaging modality is
therefore neither better nor worse than specializing by task, or not
specializing at all. Two modality adapters train on more than one task's
data: microscopy holds counting and bone-marrow classification, and X-ray
holds report generation together with multi-label, dental classification
and bone-resorption regression.

\textbf{By dataset (E5).} Splitting classification further, into one adapter
per dataset, lowers balanced accuracy on both backbones
(Table~\ref{tab:e5}): by 0.035 against Shared on Qwen ($p=0.010$) and by
0.059 on MedGemma ($p=0.001$); against TaskLoRA-cls the drop is 0.054 on
MedGemma ($p=0.002$) and 0.022 on Qwen, where it is not significant
($p=0.10$). The loss sits in the small datasets: bone marrow ($-0.155$ Qwen,
$-0.216$ MedGemma against TaskLoRA-cls; 95\% CIs exclude zero, not significant
after Holm across the ten datasets), BUS-UCLM and retinography. Large
datasets (CMMD, dental) are unchanged, and IUGC gains slightly. Across the
ten datasets the gain over TaskLoRA-cls tends to grow with training size on
MedGemma (Spearman $\rho=0.60$, permutation $p=0.08$; Qwen $\rho=0.16$,
$p=0.65$) and does not follow zero-shot difficulty ($\rho=0.35$ and $0.02$).
Classification datasets thus benefit from being trained together, and
cutting sharing below the task level costs the smaller ones.

One observation already shapes the discussion: in our challenge entry,
task-specific adapters outperformed a shared adapter, but they also had
task-specific prompts and per-task tuning (for example, class rebalancing)
that the shared adapter did not, and used a different recipe. Under a
matched protocol with bare prompts the two are close on Qwen. Comparisons of sharing strategies are
therefore sensitive to confounds in prompts and tuning, which a controlled
protocol must remove.


% ============================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Two backbones, which differ at once in size, input resolution
    ($768^2$ cap versus a fixed $896^2$) and pretraining domain.
    \item One benchmark-derived data collection, not prospective clinical
    data; possible overlap between backbone pretraining data and the
    evaluation datasets.
    \item One fixed holdout. A two-fold cross-validation design was dropped
    for compute; three seeds and bootstrap intervals cover part of that
    variance~\cite{bradshaw2023crossvalidation}.
    \item Small cells (counting $n=100$, bone-resorption regression $n=70$,
    BUS-UCLM detection $n=22$) give wide intervals.
    \item One recipe for every arm, without per-method tuning; a method
    could do better with its own tuning.
    \item Automated report evaluation (CRIMSON) and a coarse, folder-based
    definition of modality.
    \item Report generation has one dataset, so its task macro is a single
    dataset.
\end{itemize}

\section{Supplementary Studies (If Time Permits)}
\label{sec:supplementary}

The studies below were in the original design but are outside the main
plan because of compute. They are run only if time remains after E0--E9,
and are reported as supplementary, not as main claims.

\subsection{Soft Adapter Composition}

Combine task-specific adapters with fixed or learned weights,
\begin{equation}
\Delta W(x,q)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k(x,q)\,\Delta W_k,
\end{equation}
and test whether the benefit of combining two adapters follows their direct
transfer score $C_{ij}$ (E3). Task + modality composition,
$\Delta W(x,q)=\Delta W_t+\lambda_m\Delta W_m$, belongs here too.

\subsection{Latent Experts and Routing}

Train a LoRA mixture-of-experts with a learned router,
\begin{equation}
\Delta W(x,q)=\sum_{k\in\mathrm{TopK}(g(h))}\pi_k(h)\,B_kA_k,
\end{equation}
and spectral expert variants, and test whether the router discovers task
structure without task labels. Measures: expert utilization, routing
entropy, task-conditioned expert probabilities and the routing distance
between tasks,
\begin{equation}
D_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|p(k\mid t_i)-p(k\mid t_j)\right|.
\end{equation}
A router perturbation, $\tilde{\pi}=(1-\lambda)\pi+\lambda u$ with $u$ the
uniform distribution, tests how strongly predictions depend on the learned
routes.

\subsection{Specialization Location}

Place the specialized parameters in the vision encoder, the multimodal
projector, the late language-model blocks, or the LoRA adapters, and compare
downstream performance and representation shift: which parts of the VLM can
be specialized while preserving its pretrained multimodal representations?

\subsection{Practical Adaptation Factors}

Interactions of the sharing arms with image resolution, LoRA rank, class
rebalancing, image preprocessing, data augmentation, multi-image handling,
prompting and decoding. Our challenge experiments touched several of these
but under different protocols, so they would need to be rerun under the
protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup}.
